<SectionIntro level="N4">
In English, we simply add "can" or "be able to" before a verb. In Japanese, we *conjugate* the verb into the **Potential Form** (可能形 - kanoukei). This form is used to express both personal ability (skills you possess) and situational possibility (things conditions allow you to do). Let's unlock the power of "can"!
</SectionIntro>

## 1. Using できる for Nouns and Group 3

Before we look at conjugation, it is easiest to start with the verb `できる` (to be able to do/to be possible). 

If a noun represents an action or a skill (like "tennis", "driving", "Japanese"), you can simply say:
`[Noun] が できる`

<GrammarBox client:load 
  pattern="[Noun] が できる"
  meaning="To be capable of [Noun] / to be able to do [Noun]."
  formation="Action Noun + が + できる"
  examples={[
    { japanese: "私は日本語ができます。", romaji: "watashi wa nihongo ga dekimasu.", translation: "I can speak/use Japanese." },
    { japanese: "田中さんはテニスができません。", romaji: "Tanaka-san wa tenisu ga dekimasen.", translation: "Mr. Tanaka cannot play tennis." }
  ]}
/>

For **Group 3 Verbs** (Suru verbs), the potential form of `する` is simply `できる`. The potential form of `来る` (kuru) is `来られる` (korareru).

<ExampleSentence client:load 
  japanese="明日、パーティーに来られますか。"
  romaji="ashita, paatii ni koraremasu ka."
  translation="Can you come to the party tomorrow?"
/>

## 2. Conjugating Group 1 (U-Verbs)

For Group 1 verbs, drop the final `〜u` sound and change it to the `〜eru` sound. In hiragana terms, change the `U-row` character to the `E-row` character, and add `る`.

**Examples:**
* 書く (kaku) → 書ける (kakeru) : Can write
* 泳ぐ (oyogu) → 泳げる (oyogeru) : Can swim
* 飲む (nomu) → 飲める (nomeru) : Can drink
* 買う (kau) → 買える (kaeru) : Can buy

<GrammarBox client:load 
  pattern="[Group 1 Verb Root] + える (eru)"
  meaning="To be able to do [Group 1 action]."
  formation="u-row character ➔ e-row character + る"
  examples={[
    { japanese: "私はたくさんビールを飲めます。", romaji: "watashi wa takusan biiru o nomemasu.", translation: "I can drink a lot of beer." },
    { japanese: "彼は漢字が書けません。", romaji: "kare wa kanji ga kakemasen.", translation: "He cannot write kanji." },
    { japanese: "この川で泳げますか。", romaji: "kono kawa de oyogemasu ka.", translation: "Can you swim in this river?" }
  ]}
/>

<ExampleSentence client:load 
  japanese="日本料理が一人で作れます。"
  romaji="nihon ryouri ga hitori de tsukuremasu."
  translation="I can make Japanese food by myself."
/>

> [!IMPORTANT]
> When a verb changes to the potential form, the direct object particle **を** usually changes to **が**. 
> Example: 漢字**を**書く (I write kanji) ➔ 漢字**が**書ける (I can write kanji).

## 3. Conjugating Group 2 (Ru-Verbs)

For Group 2 verbs, drop the `る` and add `られる`. 

**Examples:**
* 食べる (taberu) → 食べられる (taberareru) : Can eat
* 見る (miru) → 見られる (mirareru) : Can see
* 起きる (okiru) → 起きられる (okirareru) : Can wake up

<GrammarBox client:load 
  pattern="[Group 2 Verb Stem] + られる"
  meaning="To be able to do [Group 2 action]."
  formation="Drop る ➔ Add られる"
  examples={[
    { japanese: "私は納豆が食べられます。", romaji: "watashi wa nattou ga taberaremasu.", translation: "I can eat natto." },
    { japanese: "明日は早く起きられません。", romaji: "ashita wa hayaku okiraremasen.", translation: "I cannot wake up early tomorrow." },
    { japanese: "日本語の映画が見られます。", romaji: "nihongo no eiga ga miraremasu.", translation: "I can watch Japanese movies." }
  ]}
/>

<ExampleSentence client:load 
  japanese="このアパートでペットが飼えますか。"
  romaji="kono apaato de petto ga kaemasu ka."
  translation="Can I keep a pet in this apartment? (Note: Kau is Group 1, but this shows situational possibility)."
/>

> [!NOTE]
> In modern casual Japanese, many people drop the `ら` in Group 2 potential verbs (known as "ra-nuki kotoba"). For example, saying `食べれる` instead of `食べられる`. For the JLPT N4, you must know the proper `られる` form.


## 4. Ability vs. Possibility

The potential form in Japanese seamlessly covers two different English concepts:
1. **Ability (能力):** The physical or mental skill you possess. (e.g., I can swim 100 meters).
2. **Possibility/Situational (状況可能):** What the environment allows you to do. (e.g., I can buy a ticket here).

<ExampleSentence client:load 
  japanese="私の弟はまだ自転車に乗れません。"
  romaji="watashi no otouto wa mada jitensha ni noremasen."
  translation="My younger brother cannot ride a bicycle yet. (Ability)"
/>

<ExampleSentence client:load 
  japanese="この図書館では、映画が見られます。"
  romaji="kono toshokan de wa, eiga ga miraremasu."
  translation="In this library, you can watch movies. (Possibility/Situation)"
/>

<ExampleSentence client:load 
  japanese="このカードでチケットが買えます。"
  romaji="kono kaado de chiketto ga kaemasu."
  translation="With this card, you can buy a ticket. (Possibility/Situation)"
/>

## 5. Cultural Insight: Expressing Humility with Ability

In Japanese culture, explicitly stating "I can do [X]" (〜ができる) can sometimes sound a bit boastful or overly direct. When Japanese people talk about their own skills, especially in formal or semi-formal situations, they often use hedging words like `少し` (a little) or `まだまだ` (not yet/still a long way to go) to show humility.

For example, if someone asks "Can you speak Japanese?", a humble response would be:
`「はい、少しだけ話せます。」` (Yes, I can speak just a little.)
Even if the speaker is quite fluent, they might avoid saying `「はい、ペラペラです」` (Yes, I am fluent).

Conversely, when talking about others, the potential form is a great way to show respect for their accomplishments.
